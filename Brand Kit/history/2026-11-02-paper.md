# Tanmay Builds: editing brand kit

Derived on 2 November 2026 from every one-second frame of `References/Recording 2026-11-02 205203.mp4` (37 frames spanning 0–36 seconds). All four contact sheets were visually examined. `Approved Videos` contains no finished edits yet, so this is the observed reference style, not a claim that it has been validated against approved work.

## Visual identity

Warm paper, strong black typography, yellow graphic accents, and clean editing-system diagrams. The footage remains human and direct; graphics explain the sentence being spoken. Use one coherent visual system across the whole edit.

Approximate colors inferred from the compressed reference (238x430; not original design tokens):

| Role | Color | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paper | #E9E3D5 | Main graphics canvas and matte |
| Ink | #161511 | Headlines, rules, diagrams |
| Signal yellow | #F2DC20 | Active node, emphasized number, selected element |
| White | #FFFFFF | Captions on footage |
| Quiet grey | #8C887F | Secondary diagram labels and inactive text |

The reference demonstrates yellow as its primary accent. If a second caption emphasis color is needed, use ink on a yellow/ivory caption plate; do not invent a new hue. Captions on footage remain white with yellow highlights and a dark readability treatment. Keep any two-color emphasis pattern deterministic.

## Typography

Heavy, compact grotesk headlines with short, emphatic phrases. The reference itself shows an Archivo variable font specimen around 10 seconds; exact font files and weights cannot be verified from this recording. Use Archivo/Archivo Black if available, or a deliberately checked heavy sans-serif substitute. Use a restrained sans-serif for captions and tiny production labels; mono only for actual timecode or technical annotation. No thin decorative type.

At 1080x1920: start headlines at 84–112 px, captions at 52–64 px, and useful secondary labels at 28–36 px. Tiny source-style technical labels are decorative and must not carry essential information. Keep captions to 2–6 spoken words per group and two lines at most.

## Layout grammar observed

- 0–4 s: close speaker hook, occasional large typographic reveal layered with footage; subtle frame/selection guides.
- 5–14 s: speaker in an upper landscape window on paper; lower area becomes a modular grid of qualities, diagrams, swatches, or timeline cards. Graphics progress with the speech.
- 15–17 s: return to a tight speaker shot with one large emphasized comparison/number.
- 18–23 s: editorial process sequence: assets → composition → animation → render. Thin grid, cutout thumbnails, layer stack, and sparse yellow keyframes.
- 24–26 s: full speaker for a conversational pivot.
- 27–31 s: paper canvas, layered media/deconstructed timeline, then real software B-roll.
- 32–36 s: return to speaker, then a compact comment/CTA card.

This reference uses changing layouts, not a permanent 60/40 split. For edits matched to it, alternate full portrait speaker, upper speaker/lower explanatory panel, and full asset/demo beats. Do not force the unrelated default split from another style.

## Framing and safe areas

Keep natural face proportions and useful gestures. Use modest punch-ins as emphasis rather than constant zoom. Frame eyes and mouth away from the right social action rail and bottom description area. At 1080x1920, use an internal critical-content guide around x=72–900, y=180–1560; this is an editing guide, not a guaranteed platform safe area. Captions must not cover the mouth or key UI.

## Motion and pacing

Use short, clean reveals and spatial continuity: grid cells fill, layers separate, keyframes appear, a diagram progresses, or a real UI crop moves to the relevant control. Build one meaningful visual change roughly every 1.5–3 seconds when the sentence supports it. Let readable diagrams settle. Reserve large type for hook, main claim, and payoff. Scene transitions must preserve the speech rhythm; do not add blank intervals for an animation.

The one-second frame study supports these visual observations, but cannot measure subsecond easing, exact sound-effect timing, or prove audio/lip synchronization. Those require checks on the current source and final export.

## Footage, assets, and captions

Use supplied product captures and screenshots as evidence for the claim at that moment. Keep code and UI legible by cropping to a relevant region rather than shrinking an entire desktop. Avoid unrelated stock footage and fabricated product results. Keep all original footage intact and store edits separately.

Caption the actual speech, including product names and code-switching. Check technical spellings against supplied assets. Keep caption onset aligned to the final edited voice track. Never leave source timestamps unchanged after cuts.

## Dialogue synchronization rule

Treat original camera audio as the sync reference, then measure the supplied replacement voiceover's lag at multiple positions. Correct any constant offset or drift before making cuts. Use the same source in/out map for video and corrected dialogue. Preserve consonants and complete words; retain natural breaths. Do not claim zero delay solely because streams start at zero. If different spoken timing requires retiming, document it and verify the result at every cut and representative speech onsets.

## Review dashboard

Use the same ivory, ink, and yellow grammar. One chronological card per speech beat, with a playable synchronized preview, exact caption copy, selected B-roll/source ranges, asset thumbnails, and a clear Approve / Reject / Feedback workflow. Persist decisions locally, never silently treat unreviewed beats as approved, and keep feedback available after reload.

## Avoid

Rainbow captions; constant shakes; illegible desktop screenshots; stock visuals unrelated to speech; overly small production labels as primary information; default purple/blue SaaS styling; transitions that delay dialogue; independent audio and video cuts; copying the reference mascot as if it were an owned brand asset.
